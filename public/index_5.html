Hypothesis Testing

A method for deciding whether a result is bigger than chance alone would produce — and the p-value, which is the most useful and most misquoted number in statistics.

Fundamentals

The courtroom logic behind every statistical test, what a p-value actually says, and the two ways a test can be wrong.

From Concepts 3 and 4

Two things carry forward and do all the work. Sample means vary from sample to sample, and the standard error measures how much. A confidence interval turns an estimate into a range of plausible values. This concept asks a different question of the same machinery: not "which values are plausible" but "is what I am seeing bigger than ordinary sampling variation".

The problem

A class taught by a new method averages 74.6 marks. The old average was 70. Did the method work?

You cannot answer by looking. From Concept 3 you know that a sample of students would land somewhere near 70 even if the method changed nothing at all — sampling variation alone produces gaps of a few marks routinely. So the real question is:

If the method changed nothing, how often would ordinary variation hand me a gap this large? If the answer is "often", the gap proves nothing. If the answer is "almost never", something other than chance is at work.

The courtroom logic

A statistical test borrows its structure directly from a criminal trial, and the analogy is worth taking seriously because it explains several things that otherwise look like pedantry.

In courtIn a test
The accused is presumed innocentAssume nothing is going on — no effect, no difference
The prosecution presents evidenceYou collect data
Is the evidence strong enough to overturn the presumption?Would data like this be surprising if nothing were going on?
Verdict: guilty, or not guiltyVerdict: reject the assumption, or fail to reject it
"Not guilty" is not the same as "innocent""Fail to reject" is not the same as "no effect exists"

New words

Null hypothesis
Written H₀. The sceptical starting position: nothing is going on. The new method makes no difference, the two groups are the same, the machine is correctly set. This is what you assume true until the evidence forces you to abandon it.
Alternative hypothesis
Written H₁. The claim you suspect might be true: the method helps, the groups differ, the machine has drifted. You never try to prove this directly — you build a case against H₀ instead.
Test statistic
A single number summarising how far your data sits from what H₀ predicts, measured in standard errors. A test statistic of 3 means your result is three standard errors from where H₀ said it should be.

💡 Why the roundabout route?

It seems perverse to assume the opposite of what you believe. The reason is that H₀ is a specific claim and H₁ is a vague one. "The method adds nothing" pins down exactly what the data should look like, so you can compute how likely your result was. "The method helps" does not say by how much, so there is nothing to compute. You can only measure surprise against a specific prediction, and H₀ is the only one available.

The five steps

1

State H₀ and H₁

Before looking at the data. H₀ is always the "nothing is happening" version.

2

Choose α

How much risk of a false alarm you will accept. Conventionally 0.05.

3

Collect the data

And compute the test statistic — how many standard errors from H₀ you landed.

4

Find the p-value

How often ordinary variation would produce a result this extreme if H₀ were true.

5

Decide and report

Compare p against α, state the verdict, and give the size of the effect.

The p-value

New word

p-value
The probability of getting a result at least as extreme as the one you got, assuming H₀ is true. That last clause is not decoration — it is the whole definition, and dropping it produces every one of the common misreadings.

Read it as a measure of surprise. A small p-value says: if nothing were going on, data like mine would almost never appear — so perhaps something is going on. A large p-value says: if nothing were going on, data like mine would turn up all the time — so this proves nothing either way.

New word

Significance level, α
The threshold you choose before collecting data, below which you will treat the result as evidence against H₀. Conventionally 0.05, which means accepting a 1-in-20 chance of raising a false alarm. It is a convention, not a law of nature.

What a p-value is not

Every one of these is wrong, and all five are said routinely, including by people who should know better. Take p = 0.03:

  • "There is a 3 percent chance H₀ is true." No. The p-value is computed assuming H₀ is true — it cannot then tell you the chance that it is. The direction of the reasoning runs the other way.
  • "There is a 97 percent chance the effect is real." No, for the same reason.
  • "The effect is large." No. A p-value says nothing whatsoever about size. A tiny effect measured on a huge sample gives a very small p.
  • "p = 0.06 means there is no effect." No. It means this data was not surprising enough to overturn the assumption. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
  • "p = 0.03 is three times more convincing than p = 0.09." No. p-values are not on a linear scale of evidence and do not divide like that.

The safe habit: whenever you say a p-value out loud, say the phrase "assuming there is no effect" in the same breath. Nearly every misreading dies on contact with that clause.

You are deciding from a sample, so you can be wrong — and there are exactly two ways, which are not interchangeable.

New word

Power
The chance of detecting an effect that is genuinely there, equal to 1 − β. A test with 80 percent power finds a real effect four times in five and misses it the fifth time. Power rises with the sample size and with the size of the effect.

Drag α down to 0.01 to reduce false alarms and watch β rise — you catch fewer real effects. Drag it up and the reverse happens. The two cannot both be reduced by moving the threshold; the only way to improve both at once is a larger sample, which pushes the curves apart.

★ Which error is worse is not a statistical question

It depends entirely on what happens next, and the person who knows that is the doctor, the engineer or the manager — not the statistician.

  • Screening for a serious illness. A miss means an untreated patient; a false alarm means one more test. Type II is far worse, so use a larger α and accept more false alarms.
  • A spam filter. A false alarm sends a real email to the junk folder; a miss lets one advert through. Type I is worse, so use a small α.
  • A drug approval. A false alarm puts an ineffective drug on the market. Regulators use a small α deliberately.

Running a test: the t-test

The t-test is the workhorse for comparing means, and it comes in two forms that cover most of what you will need.

FormThe questionH₀In Python
One-sampleDoes this group's mean differ from a target value?The true mean equals the targetttest_1samp(data, target)
Two-sampleDo these two groups have different means?The two true means are equalttest_ind(a, b)
 
# One-sample: did the new teaching method beat the old average of 70?
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

scores = np.array([72, 75, 68, 80, 78, 74, 71, 77, 69, 82, 73, 76])

t, p = stats.ttest_1samp(scores, 70)
print(f"sample mean = {scores.mean():.2f}")
print(f"t = {t:.3f},  p = {p:.4f}")
sample mean = 74.58
t = 3.715,  p = 0.0034

p = 0.0034 is well below 0.05. If the true mean really were 70, a class this far above it would appear about 3 times in 1,000. That is surprising enough to abandon H₀ — and the mean of 74.6 tells you the direction and the size.

# Two-sample: did the workshop group outperform the others?
group_a = np.array([72, 75, 68, 80, 78, 74, 71, 77])
group_b = np.array([65, 70, 62, 68, 72, 66, 69, 64])

t, p = stats.ttest_ind(group_a, group_b)
print(f"A mean = {group_a.mean():.1f},  B mean = {group_b.mean():.1f}")
print(f"t = {t:.3f},  p = {p:.5f}")
A mean = 74.4,  B mean = 67.0
t = 4.026,  p = 0.00125

A 7.4-mark gap with p = 0.00125. Gaps this large would arise from sampling variation about once in 800 times if the two groups were really the same, so the difference is worth taking seriously.

💡 Other tests you will hear named

The t-test is one of a family, all following the same five steps and differing only in what they compare. The z-test is used when the population's spread is known or the sample is very large. The chi-square test handles counts falling into categories rather than means. ANOVA compares more than two groups at once. You do not need any of them yet — recognising that they share the same logic is enough, and the t-test is what this concept covers properly.

How this connects to confidence intervals

The test and the interval from Concept 4 are two views of the same calculation, and each answers a question the other cannot.

Hypothesis testConfidence interval
AnswersIs there an effect?How big is the effect, plausibly?
Gives youA yes-or-no verdict and a p-valueA range of values
WeaknessSays nothing about sizeGives no single verdict

★ The rule that links them

For a difference between two groups, tested at α = 0.05:

  • If the 95 percent interval for the difference excludes zero, then p < 0.05 — the test rejects H₀.
  • If the interval includes zero, then p ≥ 0.05 — the test does not reject H₀.

They cannot disagree, because both are built from the same estimate and the same standard error. Zero is the value H₀ claims, so asking "does the plausible range include what H₀ claims?" and "is our result surprising under H₀?" are the same question in different words.

Why the interval is usually the better thing to report

Consider a test on two million users that finds a conversion improvement of 0.02 percentage points, with p < 0.001. The result is real — with a sample that large, even a tiny effect is detectable.

And it is worthless. Nobody should rebuild a product for 0.02 points. The p-value cannot tell you that, because it measures surprise rather than size. The confidence interval — say 0.015 to 0.025 points — makes the uselessness immediately visible. Statistical significance and practical importance are different things, and only one of the two numbers shows you both.

Two more traps worth knowing about

  • Testing many things at once. Run 20 tests at α = 0.05 on data where nothing at all is going on, and you should expect about one "significant" result purely by chance. Choosing the interesting comparison after seeing the data is the same problem wearing a disguise.
  • Stopping when the answer looks good. Checking a running experiment repeatedly and halting the moment p drops below 0.05 badly inflates the false-alarm rate. Fix the sample size in advance, or use a method designed for repeated checking.
What comes after this. Everything so far has compared means or percentages. Both use hypothesis tests internally, so the vocabulary from this concept reappears there, applied to a different question.

How the concept is used in data science

Six places where a test decides whether a result is acted on — and one where the test is the wrong tool.

★ The thread running through all six

A test answers one narrow question: is this bigger than chance? It never answers "is it big enough to matter", "is it worth the cost", or "did I measure the right thing". Those remain yours to answer, and the commonest failure in practice is treating a small p-value as though it had settled all four.
