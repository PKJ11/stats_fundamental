Sampling and Sampling Distributions

How to take a sample worth trusting, what happens to a number when it is computed from one, and how to turn a single answer into an honest range.

Fundamentals

Concept 3 showed that sample means behave predictably. This one shows how to collect a sample properly in the first place, and what to do with the result.

From Concept 3

Three things carry forward. A sample is the part of the population you measured. The sample means from many samples form a bell shape centred on the population mean. And their spread is the standard error, equal to the population's standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size. Everything here builds on those.

Where this concept sits

Concept 3 answered a question about behaviour: what happens to sample means if you take lots of samples? That is a fact about the world, and you never actually take lots of samples.

In real work you take one. This concept is about the two practical questions that raises — how do I take that one sample so it is worth trusting, and what may I honestly claim from it.

Part 1 · Taking a sample worth trusting

New words

Sampling frame
The list you actually draw your sample from — the electoral roll, the customer database, the register of households in a ward. It is meant to be a complete list of the population, and when it is not, everybody it leaves out is invisible to your study no matter how large a sample you take.
Bias
A systematic tendency to land on the wrong answer. Not bad luck — bad method. A biased sample gives an answer that is wrong in the same direction every time you repeat it, so collecting more data does not help.
Representative
A sample is representative when its make-up resembles the population's. The point of a good sampling method is to make this likely without you having to arrange it by hand.

Five ways to draw a sample

MethodHow it worksWhen it is usedWhat can go wrong
Simple random Every member of the population has an equal chance of being picked, like drawing names from a hat The default, and the one all the mathematics assumes Needs a complete list to draw from, which is often the hard part
Systematic Pick every 10th name from the list, starting at a random point Easy to carry out on the ground — every 10th shopper, every 20th part If the list has a repeating pattern that matches your interval, you lock onto it
Stratified Split the population into groups first — by city, by year of study — then take a random sample within each When you want every group properly represented, especially small ones You need to know the group sizes in advance to get the proportions right
Cluster Split into natural groups, pick a few groups at random, and survey everybody in those When travel or access is expensive — pick 20 villages, visit every house in them People within one cluster tend to resemble each other, so it is less precise than it looks
Convenience Whoever is easy to reach — the students in your class, the people who answer the form Quick, cheap, and almost never trustworthy Produces bias you cannot measure or correct. Included here so you can recognise and avoid it

The one that ruins everything

Every method above except the last aims at the same thing: giving each member of the population a known, fair chance of being picked. That is what the mathematics of Concept 3 assumes, and it is what a convenience sample abandons.

The damage is worse than it appears. A small fair sample gives a rough answer with a large margin, and you know it is rough. A large unfair sample gives a wrong answer with a small margin — and it looks authoritative. Size cannot fix a bad method; it only makes the wrong answer more confident.

Two different ways to be wrong

It helps to separate the two problems, because only one of them is fixed by collecting more data.

Each panel shows where twelve samples landed. The centre of the target is the truth. Bias moves the whole cluster off-centre; variability spreads it out. More data tightens the cluster — it never moves it back to the middle.

Part 2 · What happens to a number computed from a sample

New words

Estimate
Any number you compute from a sample in order to say something about the population. A sample mean is an estimate of the population mean; a sample percentage is an estimate of the population percentage.
Sampling distribution
The pattern an estimate would form if you took sample after sample and wrote down the answer each time. You met this in Concept 3 for the sample mean without it having a name. Every estimate has one.

The idea in one line

An estimate computed from a sample is itself a quantity that varies. It has a centre, it has a spread, and it has a shape — exactly like any column of data. The sampling distribution is that centre, spread and shape, and knowing it is what lets you say how much to trust the one estimate you actually have.

The two you will use constantly

EstimatingCentred onStandard errorShape
A mean — average height, average bill The population mean σ ÷ √n Bell, for n of about 30 or more
A percentage — share who say yes, click rate The population percentage √( p(1−p) ÷ n ) Bell, when both n×p and n×(1−p) are above about 10

💡 Why a percentage needs its own formula

A percentage is really a mean in disguise — count each "yes" as 1 and each "no" as 0, and the average of those is the proportion saying yes. So the same ÷ √n logic applies.

What is different is that you do not need a separate standard deviation. For a yes-or-no quantity the spread is decided entirely by p itself, which is why the formula contains only p and n. It is largest at p = 0.5 — opinion split down the middle is the hardest case to measure precisely, which is why polls quote their margin for a 50-50 split.

Part 3 · From one number to an honest range

You have a sample mean of 74.6. You know it is not the population mean, and you know roughly how far off it is likely to be. So report a range rather than a number.

New word

Confidence interval
A range built around your estimate, wide enough that ranges built this way capture the true value a stated share of the time. A 95 percent confidence interval is built so that 95 percent of such intervals contain the truth.

★ How to build one

It is one line of arithmetic, and every piece of it you already have:

estimate ± ( multiplier × standard error )

The multiplier comes from the bell shape and the 68–95–99.7 rule of Concept 2. For 95 percent confidence it is 1.96, which is where the "about 2 standard deviations" of that rule comes from — 2 was the rounded version all along.

ConfidenceMultiplierMeaning
90 percent1.645Narrower range, misses more often
95 percent1.96The usual choice
99 percent2.576Wider range, misses less often

The interpretation trap

Here is the sentence almost everybody gets wrong. A 95 percent confidence interval of 72 to 77 does not mean "there is a 95 percent chance the true value is between 72 and 77".

The true value is a fixed number. It is either inside your interval or it is not — there is no chance about it, you simply do not know which. What the 95 percent describes is the procedure: if you repeated the whole exercise many times, about 95 percent of the intervals you built would contain the truth.

The widget above makes it concrete. Before you draw a sample, there is a 95 percent chance you will get one of the good intervals. Once it is drawn and sitting on the page, it either contains 50 or it does not. Look at the red lines — nothing about them looks any different from the others.

What makes an interval wider or narrower

ChangeEffect on the intervalWhy
Larger sampleNarrowerThe standard error falls with √n
More variable populationWiderA larger σ on top of the standard error
Higher confidenceWiderA larger multiplier — certainty is bought with vagueness

That last row is the trade nobody escapes. You can have a narrow interval or a high chance of being right, and moving one moves the other. A 100 percent confidence interval would run from minus infinity to plus infinity — perfectly reliable and perfectly useless.

💡 One honest caveat about the multiplier

The multiplier of 1.96 assumes you know the population's standard deviation. In practice you almost never do — you estimate it from your own sample, which adds a little extra uncertainty that 1.96 does not account for. With a large sample the difference is negligible. With a small one, the correct multiplier is somewhat larger: for a sample of 12 it is about 2.20 rather than 1.96, making the interval a little wider. That adjustment comes from the t distribution mentioned in Concept 2, and it is covered properly later. Until then, treat intervals from small samples as slightly optimistic.

All of it in Python

# a confidence interval for a mean, built from the pieces
import numpy as np

scores = np.array([72, 75, 68, 80, 78, 74, 71, 77, 69, 82, 73, 76])

n    = len(scores)
mean = scores.mean()
sd   = scores.std(ddof=1)          # n-1, as in Concept 1
se   = sd / np.sqrt(n)             # the standard error

lo, hi = mean - 1.96*se, mean + 1.96*se
print(f"mean {mean:.2f}   se {se:.2f}   95% interval {lo:.2f} to {hi:.2f}")

# and for a percentage
n_asked, n_yes = 400, 248
p  = n_yes / n_asked
se = np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n_asked)
print(f"{p*100:.1f}%   95% interval {(p-1.96*se)*100:.1f}% to {(p+1.96*se)*100:.1f}%")
mean 74.58   se 1.23   95% interval 72.17 to 77.00
62.0%   95% interval 57.2% to 66.8%
The yes-or-no version of the same question. A confidence interval answers "what values are plausible?". The next concept answers a different question about the same data: "is this result big enough that chance alone probably did not produce it?" The two turn out to be closely connected — if a range of plausible differences does not include zero, the difference is one the next concept would call real. That connection, and the vocabulary that goes with it, is the whole of Concept 5.

Six places where how the data was collected, or the range around a result, decides what can be claimed.

★ The thread running through all six

Two habits cover all of them. Ask how the data was collected before you analyse it — no calculation repairs a sample that was not fairly drawn. And report a range rather than a number, because a bare estimate hides the one thing a reader most needs to know about it.
