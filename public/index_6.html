Shape, Quartiles and Unusual Values

This concept goes back and finishes Concept 1. Cutting data into quarters, measuring lopsidedness with a number, and a defensible rule for deciding a value is unusual — all of which were promised and deferred.

Fundamentals

Four ideas held back from Concept 1, now with enough foundation under them to be worth having.

Where this fits

Concept 1 gave you six numbers and left three gaps, each marked with a note saying the answer would come later. This is later.

There was a reason for the delay and a reason for finishing now. The delay was that these ideas are easier once you have seen why they matter. The reason for finishing is that Concepts 7 and 8 need them — the methods there are wrecked by a single extreme value, and a student with no rule for spotting one can compute a result but cannot criticise it.

The three promises. A measure of spread that is not dragged by one extreme value. A single number for how lopsided a set of values is. And a defensible test for deciding whether a value sits far enough from the rest to investigate. All three are delivered in this concept, in that order.

Part 1 · Cutting the data into quarters

The median works by splitting sorted data into two halves. There is no reason to stop at two.

New words

Quartiles
The three values that cut sorted data into four equal parts. Q1 has a quarter of the data below it, Q2 is the median with half below it, and Q3 has three quarters below it.
Percentile
The same idea at any fraction you like. The 90th percentile is the value with 90 percent of the data below it. Q1 is the 25th percentile and Q3 is the 75th, so quartiles are just three particularly useful percentiles.
Five-number summary
Minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum. Five numbers that between them describe where the data sits, how spread out it is, and whether it leans to one side.

Finding them by hand

Sort the values, then find the median. Q1 is the median of the lower half and Q3 is the median of the upper half. That is the whole procedure.

One warning: different software cuts the quarters in slightly different ways when the count does not divide evenly, so pandas, Excel and a textbook can give slightly different values of Q1 for the same small dataset. The differences are tiny and vanish on any real dataset. Use .quantile() and do not worry about it.

Part 2 · The measure of spread that holds still

New word

Interquartile range, IQR
Q3 minus Q1 — the span covered by the middle half of the data. It ignores the top quarter and the bottom quarter entirely, which is exactly what makes it immune to extreme values.

This is the first promise paid. Concept 1's table had a gap in it: every measure of spread there — range, variance, standard deviation — was dragged by a single extreme value, while two of the three centre measures were not. The IQR fills that gap.

Part 3 · The box plot

The five-number summary has a standard picture, and it is the most information-dense chart in elementary statistics.

New words

Box plot
A drawing of the five-number summary. The box runs from Q1 to Q3, so it covers the middle half. The line inside the box is the median. The whiskers reach out to the furthest values that are not unusual. Anything beyond them is drawn as a separate dot.
Fences
The limits the whiskers stop at, set at 1.5 × IQR beyond each end of the box. Values outside them get plotted individually.

Part 4 · Deciding a value is unusual

Concept 1 used the phrase "unusual value" and admitted it was doing so informally. Here are the two rules that make it precise, and the reason one of them is far better than the other.

New word

Outlier
A value far enough from the rest of the data to be worth investigating. Note the wording — worth investigating, not "wrong" and not "delete this". Deciding what to do with one is a separate judgement covered further down.
RuleFlags a value whenBuilt from
The IQR ruleIt falls below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQRQuartiles — which extreme values cannot move
The standard deviation ruleIt sits more than 3 standard deviations from the meanMean and standard deviation — which extreme values can move

Both sound reasonable. The second has a flaw so serious that it can fail on exactly the data it was meant to check, and the widget below shows it happening.

Masking

An outlier inflates the mean and the standard deviation, and the standard deviation rule then uses those inflated figures to decide what counts as unusual. The outlier widens its own net until it fits inside. This is called masking, and it gets worse with more than one outlier — two extreme values inflate the spread even further and hide each other.

The IQR rule is immune because quartiles do not move when values at the ends get more extreme. Prefer the IQR rule by default. The standard deviation rule is reasonable only when you already know the data is bell-shaped and free of severe outliers — which is exactly the situation where you least need to check.

★ Finding an outlier is the beginning, not the end

The rules flag values. They never tell you what to do, and that decision needs a human who understands where the data came from:

What the value turns out to beWhat to do
Impossible — a negative age, a mark of 150 out of 100An error. Correct it if you can, otherwise remove it and say so
A recording mistake you can trace — kilograms typed where grams belongedFix it. This is the best outcome, and worth looking for
Real but from a different population — a wholesale order in a retail datasetRemove it and state the exclusion, since it answers a different question
Real, and genuinely part of what you are studyingKeep it. Use the median and IQR to summarise, and mention it in the write-up

The last row is the one most often got wrong. Deleting inconvenient values because a rule flagged them is how a genuine finding gets thrown away — the fraud that broke the pattern, the machine failure, the customer worth more than the rest combined.

Part 5 · Putting a number on lopsidedness

The second promise. Concept 1 told you to judge lopsidedness by comparing the mean against the median. That works, and it gives you a direction but not a size.

New word

Skewness
A single number measuring how lopsided a set of values is. Zero means evenly balanced. Positive means a long trail of large values on the right, which pulls the mean above the median. Negative means the trail is on the left, and the mean sits below the median.

The mean-median gap and the skewness number always agree in sign, because they are measuring the same thing. The gap is easier to compute in your head; the number is comparable across datasets with different units, which the gap is not.

💡 Reading a skewness value

SkewnessReadingWhat to do
−0.5 to 0.5Roughly balancedMean and median both fine
0.5 to 1, or −1 to −0.5Noticeably lopsidedReport both, note the gap
Beyond 1 or −1Strongly lopsidedReport the median; the mean describes few of the values

These bands are rough conventions rather than rules. Income, price, waiting time and city population data routinely score above 2.

Part 6 · Is this column bell-shaped?

The third promise, and it matters because several things you have already met assume a bell shape and quietly break without it — the 68–95–99.7 rule from Concept 2, and the standard deviation rule for outliers above.

Four checks, in order of effort

  1. Compare the mean against the median. Close together is consistent with a bell shape; far apart rules it out. Costs one line.
  2. Compute the skewness. Near zero is consistent with a bell; beyond about 1 either way is not.
  3. Count how much falls within one and two standard deviations. A bell shape gives about 68 and 95 percent. If you find 80 and 97, the data is more tightly packed in the middle with heavier ends than a bell curve has.
  4. Draw a histogram and look at it. The step people skip, and the one that catches things no summary number will — two separate humps, a hard floor at zero, a pile-up at a rounded value.

None of these is conclusive on its own. Together they are enough to decide whether the bell-shaped methods are safe to use on this column.

# the four checks, on one column
import pandas as pd

x = df['income']

print("mean   ", round(x.mean(), 2))
print("median ", round(x.median(), 2))
print("skew   ", round(x.skew(), 2))

# what fraction sits within 1 and 2 standard deviations?
m, s = x.mean(), x.std()
for k in (1, 2):
    inside = ((x > m - k*s) & (x < m + k*s)).mean()
    print(f"within {k} sd: {inside:.1%}   (a bell shape gives {'68%' if k==1 else '95%'})")

x.plot(kind='hist', bins=40)     # and then actually look at it
mean    22.91
median  20.10
skew    2.57

within 1 sd: 79.4%   (a bell shape gives 68%)
within 2 sd: 94.6%   (a bell shape gives 95%)

Every line here says the same thing. The mean sits well above the median, the skewness is far beyond 1, and too much of the data is packed within one standard deviation. This column is strongly lopsided, so the 68–95–99.7 rule and the standard deviation outlier rule should both be avoided on it.

The five-number summary is sitting inside describe() and always has been. The IQR is 34 − 19 = 15, the fences are at −3.5 and 56.5, and the mean and median are close enough to suggest a roughly balanced column. That is most of this concept read off one output.

What this sets up. The next two concepts look at two quantities together rather than one — how strongly they move in step, and how to fit a line that predicts one from the other. Both are severely distorted by a single extreme point, so the box plot and the IQR rule from this concept become the first thing you run before either of them.
