Probability Distributions

Concept 1 described the data you already have. This one describes the data you might get — and it lets you answer a question no summary number can: how likely is that?

Fundamentals

What a distribution is, the three you must know properly, and a brief look at the others you will meet by name.

From Concept 1

You need three things from the previous concept and nothing else: the mean, the standard deviation, and the idea that a standard deviation can be read as "the typical distance of a value from the mean". Those turn up constantly here. Everything else is new and is introduced as it appears.

New words

Probability
A number between 0 and 1 saying how likely something is. 0 means it never happens, 1 means it always happens, 0.5 means it happens half the time. A probability of 0.25 can also be written as 25 percent — they are the same thing.
Random variable
A quantity whose value is decided by chance: the number rolled on a die, tomorrow's rainfall in millimetres, how many customers walk in this hour. Before it happens you do not know the value; you only know which values are possible.
Probability distribution
A description of how likely each possible value of a random variable is. That is the whole idea. The rest of this concept is about the three or four shapes such a description usually takes.

Why this is the natural next step

In Concept 1 you took a column of numbers you already had and squeezed it into six summary figures. Useful — and it can only ever describe what has already happened.

A distribution does something different. It describes what might happen, which lets you answer questions the data cannot answer on its own:

  • Heights average 165 cm with a standard deviation of 7. What fraction of people are taller than 175 cm?
  • A call centre averages 4 calls an hour. What is the chance the next hour brings more than 5?
  • A student guesses at 20 multiple-choice questions. What is the chance of passing by luck alone?

None of these is answered by a mean or a standard deviation. Each needs a model of how the values spread themselves out, which is exactly what a distribution provides.

Two families: counted things and measured things

New words

Discrete
Values you can count, with nothing in between: 0, 1, 2, 3 customers. There is no such thing as 2.4 customers. Each separate value has its own probability, so a discrete distribution is drawn as a set of bars, one per value.
Continuous
Values on a sliding scale: height, weight, time, temperature. Between any two values there is always another. A continuous distribution is drawn as a smooth curve, and probability is the area underneath a section of it.

Why an exact value has no probability when the scale is continuous

Ask for the probability that somebody is exactly 170 centimetres tall — not 170.0001, not 169.9999, but exactly 170.000000… — and the answer is zero. There are infinitely many possible heights, and picking out one single point from infinitely many leaves you with nothing.

So questions about continuous quantities are always asked about ranges: what fraction are taller than 175, what fraction sit between 160 and 170. The area under the curve across that range is the answer. For counted things this problem does not arise — "exactly 3 customers" is a perfectly sensible question with a real answer.

The others, at a glance

1 · The normal distribution

The famous bell curve, and the one that turns up most often. It describes measurements that cluster around a middle value, with fewer and fewer as you move away in either direction — heights, weights, measurement errors, marks on a well-set examination.

What makes it easy to work with is that it is completely described by two numbers you already know: the mean, which says where the peak sits, and the standard deviation, which says how wide the bell is. Nothing else. Give somebody those two numbers and they can redraw your curve exactly.

★ The 68–95–99.7 rule

For any normal curve, whatever its mean and standard deviation:

  • 68% of values lie within 1 standard deviation of the mean
  • 95% lie within 2 standard deviations
  • 99.7% lie within 3 standard deviations

This is what makes the standard deviation a universal ruler. It does not matter whether you are measuring heights in centimetres or marks out of 100 — "two standard deviations out" always means the same thing, and it always covers the same 95 percent. A value sitting more than three standard deviations from the mean happens only about 3 times in 1,000 by chance, which is why such a value is worth investigating.

2 · The binomial distribution

Where the normal describes a measurement, the binomial counts how many times something happened out of a fixed number of tries. How many heads in 10 coin flips. How many of 5,000 visitors clicked. How many of 200 manufactured parts were faulty.

New words

Trial
One try with exactly two possible outcomes. One coin flip, one visitor, one part off the production line.
Success
Whichever of the two outcomes you have decided to count. It carries no sense of "good" — if you are counting faulty parts, a faulty part is a success.
Independent
One trial's outcome tells you nothing about the next. A coin has no memory of the previous flip. This one matters more than it looks, and the check below explains why.
n and p
The two numbers that describe a binomial completely: n is how many trials, p is the probability of success on any one of them.

The four checks

A situation is binomial only when all four of these hold:

  1. The number of trials is fixed and known in advance. You decided to flip 10 times before you started.
  2. Each trial is independent of the others.
  3. Each trial has exactly two outcomes. Success or failure, with nothing in between.
  4. The probability p is the same for every trial. It does not drift as you go.

Worth testing on a real case: drawing 5 cards from a deck without putting them back fails check 2, because every card removed changes the odds for the next draw. Put each card back before drawing again and it passes. That distinction is the whole of the independence check.

Set p to 0.5 and the bars are symmetric. Push p towards either end and they lean. Raise n and the whole picture starts to look remarkably like a bell — which is not a coincidence, and is the reason the normal curve turns up in so many places.

💡 The mean of a binomial is just n × p

Ten flips of a fair coin: 10 × 0.5 = 5 heads expected. Five thousand visitors with a 4 percent click rate: 5000 × 0.04 = 200 clicks expected. No calculation needed beyond a multiplication, and it is worth doing before anything else because it tells you roughly where the bars will sit.

3 · The Poisson distribution

The Poisson also counts things, but the situation is different in one crucial way: there is no fixed number of trials. You are counting events that happen over a window of time or space, at some known average rate.

Calls arriving at a helpdesk in an hour. Customers entering a shop in ten minutes. Typing errors on a page. Accidents at a junction in a month. In none of these is there a fixed n — you can count the calls that arrived, but there is no meaningful way to count the calls that did not.

New word

λ (lambda)
The average number of events per window. It is the only number a Poisson distribution needs. If a helpdesk takes 4 calls an hour on average, then λ = 4 for a one-hour window — and λ = 8 if you widen the window to two hours.

The same question asked three ways

It helps to see how one setting produces all three:

  • "How long did each customer spend in the shop?" — a measurement on a sliding scale, clustering around a middle. Normal.
  • "Of the 200 customers who came in today, how many bought something?" — a fixed 200 tries, each a yes or a no. Binomial.
  • "How many customers walked in between 3 and 4 o'clock?" — events in a window, with no fixed number of tries. Poisson.

The data is from one shop on one day. The distribution you reach for depends entirely on which question you are asking.

Computing with distributions in Python

Every distribution in the scipy.stats library offers the same three functions, so learning them once is enough:

FunctionThe question it answers
.pmf(k)For counted things: the chance of exactly k
.cdf(x)The chance of x or below
1 - .cdf(x)The chance of above x
.ppf(0.95)Working backwards: which value has 95 percent below it
 
# the three distributions, same interface each time
from scipy import stats

# normal: heights averaging 165 cm, standard deviation 7
print(stats.norm.cdf(180, 165, 7))          # chance of being under 180
print(1 - stats.norm.cdf(175, 165, 7))      # chance of being over 175
print(stats.norm.ppf(0.95, 165, 7))         # the height 95% are below

# binomial: 10 coin flips
print(stats.binom.pmf(6, 10, 0.5))          # chance of exactly 6 heads
print(1 - stats.binom.cdf(7, 10, 0.5))      # chance of 8 or more

# poisson: 4 calls an hour on average
print(stats.poisson.pmf(2, 4))              # chance of exactly 2 calls
print(1 - stats.poisson.cdf(5, 4))          # chance of more than 5

💡 The commonest mistake with counted things

"8 or more" is 1 - cdf(7), not 1 - cdf(8). The cdf includes the value you give it, so cdf(8) already counts the 8 itself and subtracting it would leave you with "9 or more". Whenever you write one of these, say the phrase aloud and check which values it should include. For measurements on a sliding scale the problem does not arise, because a single exact value contributes no area.

Where the bell curve comes from

You may have noticed something while moving the sliders. Raise n on the binomial and the bars start to look like a bell. Raise λ on the Poisson and the same thing happens. This is not a coincidence, and it is the reason the normal curve appears in places that have nothing obvious to do with bells.

There is a result that explains it, and it is one of the most useful facts in statistics. It concerns what happens when you average many values together, and it turns out to be the foundation on which several later concepts are built.

The result behind it. If you take many separate groups of values from almost any source at all — however lopsided or strange — and work out the mean of each group, those means arrange themselves into a normal curve. The rougher the original data, the more values each group needs, but it happens regardless of what the original values looked like. This is the reason the normal distribution deserves the attention it gets, and it is what makes the next few concepts possible. It has a name and a full treatment waiting in a later concept.
