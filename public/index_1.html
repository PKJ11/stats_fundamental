Measures of Central Tendency and Spread

Mean, median and mode; range, variance and standard deviation. Six numbers built from nothing but addition, division and sorting — and they sit underneath everything else in this course.

Fundamentals

Why these six numbers exist, how each one is built, and the single property that divides them into two groups.

This is the first concept in the course

Everything here is built from arithmetic you already have: adding, dividing, sorting a list, and squaring a number. Nothing else is assumed.

The question these numbers answer

A column in a spreadsheet holds ten thousand numbers. Nobody can read ten thousand numbers. So you replace them with a handful that captures the essentials, and every summary number is an attempt to answer one of two questions:

  • Where does the data sit? — the centre: mean, median, mode
  • How far apart are the values? — the spread: range, variance, standard deviation

Every one of these throws information away, and that is the whole point of a summary. The skill is knowing which information each one discards, because that decides when you can trust it.

Centre: three ways to say "typical"

MeasureHow you build itWhat it ignores
MeanAdd every value, divide by how many there areNothing — every value has a say, which is both its strength and its weakness
MedianSort the values, then take the middle oneHow big each value is. Only its position in the sorted list matters
ModeThe value that appears most oftenEverything except how often each value occurs

An important detail about the median

If there is an odd number of values, the median is simply the middle one. If there is an even number, there is no single middle, so you take the mean of the two middle values.

Nine values sorted → the median is the 5th. Ten values sorted → the median is the mean of the 5th and the 6th. Sort first, always. Forgetting to sort is the commonest mistake made with the median.

New word

Unusual value
A value sitting far away from the rest of the data — a salary of ₹250,000 in a list where everything else is near ₹45,000. Statistics has a formal name for these and a set of rules for identifying them properly, both of which come in a later concept. For now, "far away from the rest" is all you need.

★ The core idea

The mean uses every value. The median uses only position. That single difference produces everything else in this concept — including why one of them can be dragged around by a single unusual value and the other cannot.

Spread: why the centre alone is not enough

Two sets of numbers can share exactly the same mean and be nothing alike. If these were delivery times from two suppliers, you would care enormously about the difference — and a table reporting only the mean would hide it completely.

The three measures of spread

MeasureHow you build itNote
RangeLargest value minus smallest valueThe simplest of the three, and it uses only two values — so one unusual value defines it entirely
VarianceThe mean of the squared distances from the meanIts units are the data's units multiplied by themselves — "rupees squared" — which no reader can interpret
Standard deviationThe square root of the varianceBack in the original units. Read it as "the typical distance of a value from the mean"

Building the standard deviation, one step at a time

  1. Find the mean. This is the point everything else will be measured from.
  2. Subtract the mean from each value. You now have a list of distances, some positive and some negative.
  3. Square each one. This is the step that needs explaining, and it gets its own box below.
  4. Add the squares and divide. The result is the variance.
  5. Take the square root. The result is the standard deviation.

Five steps, all arithmetic. Problem 3 in the Solved Problems tab works through every one of them on a small set of numbers.

Why square the distances?

Try the obvious approach first: take each value's distance from the mean and average those distances. It fails, and it fails completely — the distances always add up to exactly zero, for any set of numbers whatsoever. The values above the mean and those below it cancel out precisely, because that is what being the mean means.

So the signs have to go. Squaring removes them, since a negative number squared is positive. It also has a useful side effect: a distance of 10 contributes 100 while a distance of 5 contributes only 25, so squaring treats one large miss as worse than two small ones.

The price is that the units are now squared and meaningless to a reader. Taking the square root at the end undoes exactly that damage, which is why the standard deviation and not the variance is what goes into a report.

💡 Divide by n or by n − 1?

When you divide the sum of squares at step 4, there are two possible divisors, and different software makes different choices. In Python, pandas' .std() divides by n − 1 while NumPy's np.std() divides by n. The same column can therefore give you two slightly different answers.

Use n − 1. Here is the reason in plain terms: your data is almost always a small part of something bigger, and you measured the distances from your own mean rather than from the true mean of the whole. Your mean sits, by construction, right in the middle of your own numbers, so those distances come out slightly too short. Dividing by n − 1 instead of n makes the answer slightly larger and corrects the shortfall.

Pass ddof=1 to make NumPy agree with pandas. The difference matters when you have very few values and all but disappears once you have a few hundred.

★ Two groups of measures

Mean, range and standard deviation are all built from the actual sizes of the values, so a single value far from the rest pulls all three.

Median and mode are built from position and from counting, so they barely notice such a value.

Neither group is better than the other — they answer different questions. A complete summary reports both, and the gap between the mean and the median is itself worth reading: when the two are close, the values are evenly balanced; when they are far apart, something is pulling hard on one side.

MeasureWhat it tells youDragged by one unusual value?
MeanCentre, using every valueYes
MedianCentre, by position in the sorted listNo
ModeThe most common value; works on words as well as numbersNo
RangeTotal spread, largest minus smallestYes, entirely
VarianceSpread, in squared unitsYes

A measure of spread that is not dragged. You may have spotted a gap in that table — every measure of spread here is pulled by an unusual value, while two of the three centre measures are not. There is a measure of spread that holds steady, built by cutting the sorted data into four equal parts instead of using every value. It needs ideas we have not covered yet, so it waits for a later concept. For now, when a column contains a value far from the rest, report the median for the centre and say plainly that the spread measures are affected by it.

All six in Python

# the six numbers from this concept, on one column
import pandas as pd

marks = pd.Series([72, 85, 90, 66, 78, 95, 58, 78])

print("mean     ", round(marks.mean(), 2))
print("median   ", marks.median())
print("mode     ", marks.mode().tolist())
print("range    ", marks.max() - marks.min())
print("variance ", round(marks.var(), 2))
print("std dev  ", round(marks.std(), 2))
mean      77.75
median    78.0
mode      [78]
range     37
variance  151.64
std dev   12.31

.mode() gives back a list rather than a single number, because a set of values can have two or more equally common values — or none at all, if every value appears exactly once.

How the concept is used in data science

These are not just numbers you print at the top of a notebook and move past. Each one below is a decision a working data scientist makes using nothing more than the six measures from the previous tab.

★ The thread running through all six

In every one of these the question is the same: does this column contain values sitting far from the rest? Compare the mean against the median to find out — it costs one line and takes seconds. If they are close, the mean and standard deviation are safe and use more of the information available. If they are far apart, lean on the median.
