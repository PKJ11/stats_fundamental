The Central Limit Theorem

Take the average of a handful of values, do it again and again, and the averages arrange themselves into a bell curve — no matter what the original values looked like. This is the result the rest of statistics is built on.

Fundamentals

Why sample averages behave so much better than the values they came from, and what that buys you.

From Concepts 1 and 2

You need the mean and the standard deviation from Concept 1, and from Concept 2 the normal curve along with the 68–95–99.7 rule — that 68 percent of values sit within one standard deviation of the mean, 95 percent within two, and 99.7 percent within three. Everything else here is new.

New words

Population
Everything you would like to know about. Every household in a city, every part the factory will ever produce, every customer the shop will ever serve. Usually far too large to measure completely.
Sample
The part of it you actually measured. Thirty households, fifty parts, two hundred customers.
Sample mean
The mean of your sample. It is not the same thing as the population mean, and the gap between the two is what this whole concept is about.

The problem this solves

You measure 40 students and find a mean mark of 63. What you actually want to know is the mean mark of all 900 students in the institute — and those two numbers are not the same. Measure a different 40 students and you would get a different answer.

So the honest question is not "what is the mean" but "how far from the truth is my answer likely to be?" Without an answer to that, a sample mean is just a number with no weight behind it.

The Central Limit Theorem answers it, and it does so in a way that seems almost too convenient — which is why it is worth watching happen rather than being told.

New word

Sampling variation
The fact that two different samples from the same population give two different answers. It is not a mistake and not carelessness — it is unavoidable, and it happens even when every sample is drawn perfectly. The whole of this concept is about measuring how large it is.

★ The Central Limit Theorem

Take many samples of the same size from any population at all, and work out each sample's mean. Those means arrange themselves into a normal curve.

Read it again, because the surprising part is easy to skim past: the shape of the original population does not matter. It can be lopsided, flat, or split into two humps. The sample means still form a bell.

This is not a rough approximation that usually works out. It is a mathematical result, and it holds for almost any population you could construct.

The three facts you actually use

The theorem is usually stated as one sentence, but it gives you three separate pieces of information, and each does a different job.

What it saysIn wordsWhat it lets you do
1. The shape The sample means form a normal curve Use the 68–95–99.7 rule on them, whatever the original data looked like
2. The centre The sample means centre on the population mean Trust that your method is not systematically too high or too low
3. The spread The sample means have a spread of σ ÷ √n Put a number on how far your one sample mean is likely to be from the truth

New word

Standard error
The standard deviation of the sample means — the third fact above. It is given by the population's standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size, written σ ÷ √n. It is called an "error" because it measures how far a typical sample mean lands from the population mean.

Be careful not to confuse it with the standard deviation. The standard deviation describes how spread out the individual values are. The standard error describes how spread out the sample means are — and it is always the smaller of the two, because averaging smooths things out.

The square root, and why it matters so much

The √n in the formula is the single most practically important part of this concept, because it governs what more data buys you.

💡 How large does n need to be?

The usual rule of thumb is n of 30 or more, and the widget above shows where it comes from — by 30 the right-hand panel looks convincingly like a bell even for the most lopsided population offered.

It is a rule of thumb rather than a law. The more lopsided the population, the larger n needs to be, and a population with a few enormous values may need considerably more than 30. If the population is already bell-shaped, on the other hand, the sample means are bell-shaped at any n at all — try it with the bell population and a sample size of 2.

Putting it to work

Here is the whole point of the concept in one worked line. A factory fills bags to a mean of 500 g with a standard deviation of 20 g. You weigh a sample of 25 bags.

  • The standard error is 20 ÷ √25 = 20 ÷ 5 = 4 grams.
  • By the 68–95–99.7 rule, about 95 percent of samples of 25 will give a mean within 2 standard errors of 500 — that is, between 492 and 508 g.
  • So if your sample of 25 comes out at 510 g, something has changed. That result is beyond where 95 percent of honest samples land.

Notice what just happened. You knew nothing about the shape of individual bag weights, you measured only 25 bags out of millions, and yet you could say something precise about how far your answer might be from the truth. That is what the theorem buys.

Where it does not apply

Three situations where reaching for the theorem would be a mistake:

  • Your sample is not drawn fairly. The theorem assumes every member of the population has an equal chance of being picked. Surveying only the people who answer the phone breaks that, and no sample size repairs it — a larger biased sample is simply a more confident wrong answer.
  • You are asking about one individual, not an average. The theorem describes sample means. It says nothing about how a single value behaves, and confusing the two is the commonest error made with it.
  • The sample is small and the population strongly lopsided. With n of 5 from a population with a few enormous values, the sample means are still visibly lopsided. Try it in the widget above.

Why this concept is the hinge of the course

Concepts 1 and 2 described data. This one is the first that lets you make a claim about something you did not measure — and almost everything that follows depends on it.

When somebody reports a survey result "give or take three percent", or says two groups differ "more than chance would explain", the reasoning underneath is the one on this page. Those methods have their own names and their own concepts ahead of them, and every one of them assumes what you have just watched happen.

What gets built on this. Two things follow directly. The first is a way of stating a result as a range rather than a single number, using the standard error to say how wide that range must be. The second is a way of deciding whether a difference between two groups is larger than sampling variation alone would produce. Both have concepts of their own ahead, and both would be impossible without the standard error you have just met.

Demonstrating it yourself in Python

# build a deliberately lopsided population, then average samples from it
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

population = rng.exponential(10, 100000)      # nothing like a bell
print("population mean", round(population.mean(), 2))
print("population sd  ", round(population.std(), 2))

for n in [1, 5, 30, 100]:
    means = [rng.choice(population, n).mean() for _ in range(2000)]
    print(f"n = {n:3d}   mean of the means {np.mean(means):6.2f}"
          f"   spread {np.std(means):5.2f}"
          f"   predicted {population.std()/np.sqrt(n):5.2f}")

Six places where the standard error, and the bell shape of sample means, decides what can honestly be claimed.

★ The thread running through all six

Each one turns a single measured number into a number with a margin attached. That margin is the standard error, and attaching it is the difference between reporting a result and reporting a fact. A figure quoted without one is a figure whose reliability nobody can judge — including the person who computed it.
